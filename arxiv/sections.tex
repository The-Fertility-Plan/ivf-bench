\textbf{The judge.} GPT-5.4 grades every response against the five rubrics in a
single call and additionally extracts the numeric probability, observing the case
text and the response but not the embryo image. This is a deliberate limitation
whose cost we state explicitly: the morphology rubric measures whether a
description is consistent with the Gardner annotation supplied to the model
rather than whether the model read the image correctly, and a judge that cannot
see the image cannot detect a fluent description of an embryo that is not
there.

\textbf{Two evaluation defects.} Two defects in our own pipeline, described in
full in Appendix~\ref{app:defects}, invalidated an earlier version of these
results: failed judge calls were persisted as all-zero score records that
subsequent runs treated as complete, and our locally served model was graded on
its chain of thought because vLLM was run without a reasoning parser, which
depressed its test-split score from 4.17 to 3.80. All results reported below
follow both corrections, so that every score in the study is produced by GPT-5.4
and no score record contains a judge failure.

\subsection{Reading the two outcome metrics}

\textbf{Brier score} (lower is better) is the mean squared error between the
stated probability and the observed outcome, and it rewards honesty about the
odds; always predicting 50\% yields 0.25, which is the comparison most papers
adopt and the wrong one, since the sharper reference is a constant set to the
cohort's own pregnancy rate, against which we report in
Table~\ref{tab:baserate}. \textbf{AUROC} (higher is better, with 0.5 denoting
chance) is the probability that a randomly chosen success is ranked above a
randomly chosen failure, and it rewards discrimination between embryos.
Counseling a patient depends on the first quantity and selecting an embryo for
transfer on the second, and a system can be respectable on one while remaining
useless on the other.

\subsection{Which gaps are real}

A leaderboard reported without intervals invites readers to treat a difference
of 0.02 as meaningful, so we resample cases with replacement 10,000 times and, for
each adjacent pair of systems, bootstrap the paired per-case difference over the
cases both answered.

On the test split every adjacent gap is separable at the 95\% level, the narrowest
being Kimi K2.5 over Sonnet 4.6 at $+0.052$ (95\% CI $[+0.005, +0.098]$,
$p{=}0.031$), whereas on the held-out split, with 103 cases rather than 550, two
gaps close: Gemini Flash over Qwen 397B ($+0.015$, CI $[-0.049, +0.082]$,
$p{=}0.66$) and Kimi K2.5 over Sonnet 4.6 ($+0.076$, CI $[-0.023, +0.169]$,
$p{=}0.12$), both of which should be read as ties. The two comparisons that carry
the paper's argument survive on both splits: GPT-5.4 over our model ($+0.44$ held
out, CI $[+0.38, +0.51]$) and our model over Opus 4.6 ($+0.10$ held out, CI
$[+0.03, +0.17]$, $p{=}0.007$).

\subsection{What the numbers say}

\textbf{1. Case explanation is uniformly strong.} Clinical integration is the
highest-scoring rubric for every system evaluated, with GPT-5.4 reaching a
maximal 5.00 on both splits; models reliably incorporate age, AMH, endometrial
thickness, and prior cycle history into a coherent account of the patient's
prognosis, such that if the task were confined to producing the counseling
narrative it would be close to solved.

\textbf{2. Outcome prediction sits at a floor, and the deficit can be stated
precisely.} AUROC spans 0.450 to 0.562 across the systems evaluated, against 0.5
for chance, so that the best value obtained, 0.562, corresponds to ranking a
conceiving embryo above a non-conceiving one on 56\% of such pairs.

The Brier scores appear more favorable than they are. They cluster between 0.241
and 0.289, which improves on the 0.25 obtained by always predicting 50\% and
invites the reading that calibration is adequate, but that is the wrong reference
point: the clinical pregnancy rate in this cohort is 0.350, so a constant
predictor that returns 35\% for every case, disregarding the embryo, the image,
and the patient entirely, scores 0.2274, and every system in the study performs
worse.

This is the result we most want a reader to retain, and it is not a verdict on
the models, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}: the
patient-context fields we supply are sampled independently of outcome and
therefore contain no signal any model could exploit, while the measured data that
remain are age, AMH, endometrial thickness, oocyte counts, and a single
morphology grade from one day-5 frame. Predicting a live birth from that
combination approaches the limit of what the public data support, and no
architectural change addresses it; the inputs that would move the number are
embryo genetics, longitudinal development rather than a single frame, uterine and
endometrial context, and the lifestyle and history data that at present never
leave the physician's notes. IVF-Bench is constructed so that these fields can be
substituted from generated to measured as they become available, which is exactly
the experiment the field is currently unable to run.

The same reasoning reframes the calibration result reported for our post-trained
model in Section~\ref{sec:orpo}: its held-out Brier of 0.245 is second of the
eight, behind Sonnet 4.6 at 0.242, and all eight fall below a constant predictor.
What post-training achieved is a model whose stated probabilities moved back
toward the population rate, correcting the overconfidence of the base model,
which on these data is the correct behavior and is not predictive skill.

\textbf{3. Morphology grounding is the weakest rubric throughout.} No system
exceeds 3.98 on morphology grounding on either split, whereas five of eight
exceed 4.30 on clinical integration on the test split, indicating that models
reason more effectively about the structured data supplied in text than about the
image itself. Because our judge does not observe the image, a system can score well on this
rubric by restating the Gardner grade it was given, so these values constitute an
upper bound on image comprehension rather than a measurement of it; the ablation
in Section~\ref{sec:ablation} measures the image's contribution directly and
shows that it is large for most systems and negligible for one.

\textbf{4. Cost and quality are only loosely coupled.} Opus 4.6 incurs \$170.43
of inference to process the 550 test cases and scores 4.01, while Gemini 2.5
Flash, the least expensive commercial system evaluated, finishes seventh on that
split and rises to fourth on held out, the largest between-split movement of any
system. Our post-trained model is excluded from this comparison because it was
served on hardware we already owned, so its marginal inference cost is not
commensurable with an API price; Section~\ref{sec:orpo} states the comparison in
the terms that are.

the image at all, which would make the vision component of this benchmark
decorative. We tested this directly by re-running four systems on the same cases
with the image removed and the attachment marker replaced, holding everything
else constant, and grading both arms with the same judge; differences are

Three findings follow. The image carries substantial information for most
systems, since removing it halves GPT-5.4's morphology grounding, from 3.94 to
1.98, and reduces Qwen 397B's by 40\%, which establishes that the vision channel
is doing real work and that the benchmark is measuring it rather than rewarding
restatement of the supplied grade.

The exception is instructive. Opus 4.6 loses only 0.14 points of morphology
grounding when the image is withheld, an order of magnitude less than GPT-5.4,
which indicates that its morphology descriptions are derived almost entirely from
the Gardner annotation in the prompt rather than from the image itself. Opus
places third overall on both splits while making the least use of the visual
input of any system tested, a dissociation that a leaderboard alone would not
reveal and that we would not have detected without this ablation.

Gemini 2.5 Flash presents the opposite pathology: its overall score is
\emph{higher} without the image ($-0.14$, $p{=}0.040$) even though its morphology
grounding falls by 0.90, so the image degrades its performance on the remaining
four rubrics by more than it contributes to the one rubric that depends on it.
For this system the visual input is, on balance, a distraction.

Taken together these results support the construction of the benchmark, in that
the morphology rubric responds to the presence of the image as it should, while
also confirming the caution attached to it in Section~\ref{sec:results}: the
absolute morphology scores remain an upper bound, since the judge cannot verify a
description against the image, but the differences between conditions are
measured directly and are large.


\subsection{Two controls on the headline claims}
\label{sec:controls}

Two objections bear directly on the results above and we address both with
additional analysis rather than argument.

\textbf{Is the ceiling really the data?} Our claim that the outcome ceiling
reflects the available data rather than model capacity rests, so far, on the
observation that the generated fields are uninformative by construction, which
says nothing about the measured ones. We therefore fit logistic regression to the
measured fields alone, namely age, AMH, endometrial thickness, oocytes retrieved,
mature oocytes, transfer day, and the three Gardner components, with missing
values imputed at the column median. Cross-validated within the 550-case split it
reaches AUROC 0.538 (95\% CI $[0.487, 0.586]$), and fit on those 550 cases and
evaluated on the 103 held out it reaches 0.608 (95\% CI $[0.484, 0.725]$), an
interval wide enough to include chance. The better-powered estimate, 0.538, sits
squarely inside the 0.450 to 0.562 range spanned by the vision-language systems,
which is the result that matters: a linear model given the same measured
covariates extracts about as much prognostic signal as the frontier systems do,
so those systems are operating near the limit of what these covariates support
rather than failing to use them.

One qualification follows from the same experiment and cuts against us. On the
held-out split the tabular model attains a Brier score of 0.2197, which does beat
the constant predictor at 0.2274, so the claim that nothing in this study beats
the base rate holds for the language models and not for a fitted tabular model.
The margin is small, the sample is 103 cases, and the cross-validated Brier on
the larger split is 0.2297, which does not beat the constant; we report the
result as it stands rather than choose the split that suits us.

\textbf{Is the post-training margin an artifact of verbosity?} The judge rewards
length, as Section~\ref{sec:robustness} quantifies, and our model was trained
toward a teacher whose responses are long, so the comparison against Opus 4.6
could plausibly be measuring output length. It is not. On the held-out split our
model's responses average 9,485 characters against Opus 4.6's 11,933, so our
model is the shorter of the two, and regressing score on length within each
system and comparing at a common length widens the margin rather than narrowing
it, from $+0.103$ raw to $+0.205$ adjusted. The verbosity confound runs against
our result, not for it.

The natural question raised by the gap between a 9B open model and a frontier
system is whether that gap reflects a capability ceiling or a deficit of
domain-specific training data, and we tested this directly.

\subsection{Building preference pairs from the benchmark}

For each of the 550 test-split cases we already had seven scored responses, and
we took the highest-scoring one as \texttt{chosen} and the lowest-scoring one as
\texttt{rejected}, giving 550 pairs with a mean score gap of 1.49 points, of
which 500 were used for training and 50 held for evaluation during the run. We then fine-tuned Qwen 3.5-9B with ORPO
\citep{hong2024orpo}, LoRA rank 16 and alpha 32 applied to all linear layers
including the vision tower, three epochs on two H100s, with the learning rate and
the preference weight chosen by an eight-trial search.

Two properties of this dataset bear on the interpretation of the result. The
pairs are drawn from the test split, so the trained model is in-domain on the
550-case leaderboard and the held-out split provides the honest measurement, and
the chosen responses approach a monoculture, since GPT-5.4 authored 460 of the
500 chosen sides in the training split, or 92\%, which means the student is
largely distilling a single teacher that also serves as the judge.

released dataset and two GPUs.

Its held-out Brier of 0.245 is second of the eight, behind Sonnet 4.6 at 0.242,
and should be read alongside Table~\ref{tab:baserate}. What the fine-tune fixed
is overconfidence: the base model's mean stated probability was 0.452 against a
true rate of 0.350, and the trained model's is 0.396. Whether the same recipe yields real predictive skill
when the context fields are measured rather than generated is the open question
this benchmark exists to make answerable.

The base model's AUROC differs sharply between splits, 0.554 on test against
0.450 on held out, but with roughly 36 positive cases in the held-out split the
standard error on that statistic is near 0.06, so the difference is not
distinguishable from sampling noise and we draw no inference from it.

The cost structure is the consideration most relevant to deployment, and it
requires care, because the two systems are not billed on the same basis. Opus 4.6
incurred \$170.43 of API inference over the 550 test cases, or \$0.310 per case,
whereas our model was served on a two-GPU instance we already operated, so its
marginal cost is GPU time rather than a per-token price: at 19 seconds per case
on the \$8.38 per hour instance used throughout this study, that is \$0.044 per
case, or roughly one seventh of the Opus figure, and it falls further under
batching. We report both bases rather than the single ratio, since the
seven-fold figure is the defensible one and a comparison against our recorded
\$7.98, which is almost entirely judge cost, would not be.

% ===== ROBUSTNESS =====
A benchmark paper should attempt to falsify its own instrument, and we report
four such checks, each of which reuses the collected responses and therefore
incurs no additional cost.

\textbf{Do the five rubrics measure five distinct constructs?} Only partly: the
mean correlation between distinct rubrics is 0.49 and the first principal
component accounts for 60\% of the variance across 4,370 judgments, indicating a
substantial general quality factor. The structure is nonetheless not degenerate,
since recommendation usefulness correlates with clinical integration at 0.69 but
with guideline alignment at only 0.28, which justifies reporting the five
separately while cautioning against treating their unweighted mean as five
independent measurements.

\textbf{Does the judge reward verbosity?} It does, and unevenly: pooled across
systems the Spearman correlation between response length and overall score is
0.61, and per system it ranges from $-0.01$ for Opus 4.6 to $+0.76$ for Gemini
2.5 Flash, which is both the lowest-scoring commercial system and the one whose
score depends most heavily on output length. Future versions of this benchmark
should control for length explicitly, and readers comparing closely spaced scores
should consider whether one system simply produced more text.

\textbf{Does patient-level leakage affect the results?} It does not: twenty-two
patients contributed two embryos each, and retaining only one embryo per patient
removes 16 cases from the test split without changing any ranking or moving any
score by more than 0.01. The defect is real and should be addressed by splitting
on patient rather than on image, but it did not contaminate these results.

\textbf{Does judge self-preference inflate our result?} We cannot exclude it,
and this remains the principal open question in the paper: GPT-5.4 serves as the
judge, produced 92\% of the chosen responses from which our model was trained,
and occupies the top of the leaderboard, so a model optimized to resemble the
judge should be expected to satisfy it. Confirming the ordering with a judge from
a different model family is the most immediate extension of this work, and until
that is done our model's second position should be read as second according to
GPT-5.4 rather than as an unconditional ranking.

% ===== LIMITATIONS =====
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The judge cannot see the embryo}, so morphology grounding measures
      consistency with the Gardner grade supplied in the prompt rather than
      fidelity to the image; the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} recovers
      the image's contribution by differencing rather than by direct
      verification, and the natural remedy is the expert-written morphological
      descriptions released by \citet{neu2026dataset}, which would allow a future
      version to score image reading against a reference.
\item \textbf{One judge, and it is also a contestant.} GPT-5.4 grades a
      leaderboard it appears on, and it wrote 92\% of the responses our model was
      trained to imitate. Scoring a subset with a judge from a different family
      would settle how much that inflates our second place. It is unfinished
      work, not a design choice.
\item \textbf{The prompt asks for implantation probability and we score against
      clinical pregnancy.} Both endpoints are computed in the code; the
      leaderboard reports clinical pregnancy. Treat the outcome metrics as a
      stress test rather than a matched forecast.
\item \textbf{Part of the patient context is generated}, for the reasons and with
      the consequences set out in Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}. The two that bind
      hardest: those fields are independent of outcome, so they cap what any
      outcome metric here can show, and they are independent of each other, so
      real clinical correlations such as PCOS with BMI are absent.
\item \textbf{One clinic, one scanner, one population.} Everything here comes from
      the Kromp release. Transfer to other labs is untested.
\item \textbf{Splits are per image rather than per patient.} Quantified above;
      fix in the next version.
\item \textbf{Small absolute sample.} 550 test cases and 103 held out is enough to
      separate most models and not enough to separate close ones, which is why
      every comparison in this paper carries an interval.
\end{itemize}
