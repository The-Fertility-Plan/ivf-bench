\begin{abstract}
Embryo selection in {IVF} still rests on morphology, and the systems built to
standardise it rank blastocysts from images alone, without the patient context
clinicians weigh. We present IVF-Bench: 753 blastocyst cases from the public
Kromp dataset, each pairing a real embryo image and its Gardner annotation with
that patient's cycle data and recorded outcome, plus patient-history fields
sampled from published distributions and labelled as generated. Models write an
open-ended assessment, scored on five clinical rubrics by a judge shown the same
embryo and on the implantation probability they state. Across eight systems,
clinical integration is uniformly strong and outcome discrimination is not:
{AUROC} never exceeds 0.562, which a logistic regression on the measured fields
alone matches, placing the ceiling in the data rather than the models.
Post-training Qwen 3.5-9B with {ORPO} on 500 benchmark-derived pairs raises it
24.6\% over its own base, a gain a second judge puts at 15.5\%; the second place
it buys, ahead of Claude Opus 4.6, holds under one judge and not the other,
because judges favour their own lineage. We release the benchmark, the model, and
all 5,194 responses with transcripts from both judges.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Few decisions in IVF carry more weight than the choice of which embryo to
transfer, and in most clinics that choice still rests on visual assessment: an
embryologist examines the blastocyst on day 5 and assigns a Gardner grade
capturing the degree of expansion, the organization of the inner cell mass, and
the cohesion of the trophectoderm \citep{gardner1999}. The grade is clinically
useful, but it is also subjective, and when embryologists at different centers
are shown the same day-5 embryos and asked to select one for transfer, their
agreement falls well short of what the confidence of the decision would
suggest \citep{storr2017}.

That variability has motivated a decade of machine learning work, nearly all of
it framed as a scoring problem in which an image or a time-lapse sequence is
mapped to a scalar and embryos are ranked by that scalar. The largest randomized
evaluation of this paradigm, a double-blind trial across 14 clinics and 1,066
patients, compared a deep learning ranker against trained embryologists and did
not demonstrate noninferiority \citep{illingworth2024}, which suggests that
further refinement of image-only ranking is unlikely to be the most productive
direction.

proposes.

We argue the limitation lies in the framing rather than the architecture. The
image captures only part of what determines whether a transfer succeeds; the rest
is patient-level, comprising ovarian reserve, endometrial receptivity, body mass
index and lifestyle, previous cycles, and the andrology profile. Most of that
resides with the treating physician and rarely reaches the embryologist at the
microscope, still less the imaging model, so a system operating on pixels alone
is not failing at prediction so much as being asked to predict without the
covariates that govern the outcome.

Vision-language models can in principle consume both modalities, but the claim
has never been testable, because embryo images are sensitive clinical records,
outcome-linked patient context more sensitive still, and the review required to
assemble the two is stringent enough that essentially every substantial dataset
in this field stays private. That, rather than model capacity, is the binding
constraint, and it motivates an instrument for measuring whether a model can use
patient context at all.

\textbf{IVF-Bench} comprises 753 blastocyst cases, each imaged on the day the
embryo reached the blastocyst stage and was transferred, 641 on day 5 and 112 on
day 4, and each pairing a real embryo image and its silver-standard Gardner
annotation with the cycle data and recorded outcomes of the same patient, together with the patient-history fields that no
public dataset supplies and that we therefore generate from published population
distributions, labeling each as generated and defending the choice in
Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}. A model is asked to describe the embryo, integrate
the patient factors, estimate an implantation probability, and recommend next
steps, and its answer is scored on five clinical rubrics while the stated
probability is compared against the recorded outcome. The generated fields are
sampled without reference to those outcomes and add no prognostic information
beyond the measured covariates, as Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} quantifies, so
every outcome-prediction result reported here is bounded by the data before any
model is involved.

We evaluate seven frontier and open-weight systems and then post-train a 9B open
model on preference pairs derived from the benchmark, obtaining the following results:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Five hundred preference pairs raise a 9B open model 24.6\% over its
      own base on the held-out split}, and a judge from a different family
      independently puts the same gain at 15.5\%. This is the paper's central
      training result, a within-model comparison no leaderboard convention can
      distort, and it says the reasoning half of embryo assessment is reachable
      far below frontier scale. The gain is also enough to place the model second
      of eight ahead of Claude Opus 4.6 at roughly one seventh of the marginal
      inference cost, but that ranking is GPT-5.4's and reverses under Sonnet 4.6,
      so we report it as a consequence rather than as the claim.
\item The improvement spreads across all five rubrics rather than concentrating
      in one, which separates a procedure that raises the assessment from one
      that optimizes a reported quantity.
\item Outcome discrimination stays weak for every system, and we quantify the
      deficit against a baseline that ignores its inputs entirely, placing the
      ceiling in the data rather than in model capacity.
\item Morphology grounding is the weakest of the five rubrics, roughly 1.4 points
      below clinical integration, and putting the judge in front of the embryo is
      what revealed it: the same responses scored half a point higher under a
      judge that could not check them.
\end{enumerate}

Building the instrument surfaced four properties of rubric-based judging that
affect any benchmark of this shape, one of which withdraws a quantitative claim
the earlier release carried. They are incidental to the argument above and are
collected in Section~\ref{sec:judgelessons}.

The practical implication is that progress in this area depends less on model
development than on the construction of a public dataset linking embryo images to
measured patient context and recorded outcomes, and IVF-Bench provides the
instrument with which the value of such a dataset can be quantified.

\section{Related work}

\textbf{Embryo selection models.} Most published systems predict a label from
pixels, whether by grading blastocysts automatically, ranking embryos within a
cohort, or estimating implantation from time-lapse sequences, and the commercial
products deployed in clinics follow the same pattern, positioned as adjunctive
scoring tools rather than autonomous decision makers; the strongest randomized
evidence for any of them is the trial discussed above \citep{illingworth2024}.
None of this work evaluates open-ended clinical reasoning, for the simple reason
that none of it produces language.

\textbf{Language about embryos.} The group that released the dataset we build on
has recently moved in this direction, publishing an expert-annotated set of
embryo images paired with natural-language morphological descriptions
\citep{neu2026dataset} together with a fine-tuned PaliGemma-2 model that generates
such descriptions \citep{neu2026invitrovision}. That work and ours are
complementary but differ in what they ask of a model: they evaluate whether a
model can describe an embryo, whereas we evaluate whether it can reason about the
patient carrying that embryo, which additionally requires the cycle data, the
history, a calibrated probability, and a recommendation. Their descriptions are
the morphology ground truth our evaluation most lacks, as we note in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\textbf{Rubric-graded evaluation of medical language models.} The practice of
scoring open-ended clinical answers against written criteria, with a language
model performing the scoring, was established at scale by HealthBench, for which
262 physicians authored 48,562 rubric criteria across 5,000 conversations
\citep{arora2025healthbench}. Our five rubrics are considerably narrower, written
for a single decision rather than for medicine in general, but the methodological
inheritance is direct, and we adopt the same principle of grading against
criteria rather than against a reference answer.

\textbf{Preference optimization.} We post-train with ORPO
\citep{hong2024orpo}, which folds the preference objective into the supervised
loss and removes the need for a separate reference model, in the family of
methods opened up by DPO \citep{rafailov2023dpo}.

\section{The benchmark}

\subsection{Where the data comes from}

Open embryo data is rare, because embryo images are sensitive clinical records
and most published systems are trained on private single-clinic collections. We
build on the Kromp blastocyst dataset \citep{kromp2023}, which is public under CC
BY 4.0 and links blastocyst images to Gardner grades, selected cycle variables,
and pregnancy outcomes. Culture ran to the blastocyst stage and transfer followed
on day 4 where blastocysts were already available and on day 5 otherwise, so each
image is taken on its own case's transfer day rather than on a fixed day for
every case; the dataset records that day and we pass it to the model. The clinical annotation file contains 754 records spanning 732 patients, one of
which references an image absent from the distributed archive, leaving 753 usable
cases from 731 patients.

Preparing it took more correction than we expected, and we document that because
anyone reusing the dataset will meet the same issues. The annotation file is
semicolon-separated, Latin-1 encoded, and had at some point been opened in a
German-locale spreadsheet, which silently turned 501 AMH values and 144
endometrial thickness values into dates, so that 1.26 appears as
\texttt{J\"an.26}; we map the month abbreviations back. Oocyte counts sometimes
appear additively, as \texttt{7+1}, which we sum, and missing values stay
missing, shown to models as ``not available'' rather than imputed. One record
carries a live birth without the clinical pregnancy that must precede it, and we
leave it unaltered, since silently repairing a source dataset is less defensible
than reporting the inconsistency.

\subsection{What a case contains}

Each case combines measured fields drawn from Kromp with generated patient
context: the measured fields are the embryo image, the Gardner grades, patient
age, AMH, endometrial thickness, oocytes retrieved, mature oocytes, transfer day,
and the three pregnancy outcomes, while the generated fields are body mass index,
basal FSH, diagnosis, stimulation protocol, previous cycles and their outcomes,
partner age, semen parameters, and a lifestyle summary.

\subsection{Why the patient context is generated, and what that costs}
\label{sec:synthetic}

An embryo image accompanied by an age does not constitute a case: a clinician
reasoning about a transfer draws on the patient's history, and a benchmark that
omits that history cannot determine whether a model uses one. No public dataset,
however, carries body mass index, lifestyle, stimulation protocol, or cycle
history alongside embryo images and outcomes. Such data exist in clinics, largely
in physicians' notes and frequently only in their recollection, and they do not
circulate: they seldom reach the embryologist at the microscope and have never
reached a public research corpus, because assembling images together with
outcome-linked patient records is precisely the combination that regulatory and
privacy review is designed to constrain.

We therefore generate these fields, sampling each from a published population
distribution, with every parameter and its source given in
Appendix~\ref{app:distributions}. Generation is deterministic per case, seeded
from a hash of the case identifier, and each case record enumerates its generated
fields explicitly, so that no result can depend on generated values being
mistaken for measured ones. Identifiers are assigned positionally over the sorted
corpus, so regeneration is exact for a fixed corpus, while changing its size or
the split proportions reassigns identifiers and therefore resamples the profiles
that depend on them.

This procedure yields a case-level reasoning task that can be constructed and
reproduced entirely from public sources, at two costs that bear directly on the
interpretation of our results. First, the generated fields are sampled without reference to the recorded
outcomes. Two of them are nonetheless conditioned on the patient's real age,
namely basal FSH and partner age, which correlate with it at 0.32 and 0.73
respectively, and each therefore functions as a weak age proxy: taken alone, and
read in the direction that makes them predictive at all, with higher values
indicating a poorer prognosis, they reach AUROC 0.527 and 0.532 against clinical
pregnancy. Because age is itself
supplied as a measured field, these fields add no information a model did not
already have, but the stronger claim that the generated context is entirely
uninformative is not one we can make. The outcome metrics in
Section~\ref{sec:results} should be read as a floor that quantifies what is
missing rather than as a verdict on what vision-language models can achieve given
measured context. Second, apart from those two dependencies and the conditioning
of previous outcomes on previous cycle count, the fields are sampled
independently of one another, so genuine clinical correlations, such as that
between polycystic ovary syndrome and body mass index, are absent, and a model
that has internalized those correlations receives no credit for them here.
Neither limitation can be remedied by a more sophisticated generator; both are
remedied by a real dataset, which is precisely the argument of this paper.

\subsection{Ethics and data use}
\label{sec:ethics}

This work is a secondary analysis of a publicly released, de-identified dataset
and required no additional review. The underlying embryo images and clinical
records were collected under approval from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of
Medicine at Johannes Kepler University Linz (Nr.\ 1238/2021) with informed
consent obtained from participants, and were released by the original
investigators under CC BY 4.0 \citep{kromp2023}; we redistribute no images and
our release regenerates every case from that source. The patient-history fields
we add are sampled from published population distributions and correspond to no
real individual.

The model released with this benchmark is a research artifact and is not a
medical device. It has no regulatory clearance, has not been evaluated in a
clinical trial, and must not be used to inform the care of any patient. We regard
the risk of misuse as material rather than hypothetical, because the model
produces fluent, confident, guideline-shaped counseling text whose surface
quality substantially exceeds its demonstrated prognostic validity, which is the
combination most likely to induce unwarranted confidence in a reader. The
strongest randomized evidence in this area found that a deep learning system did
not outperform trained embryologists \citep{illingworth2024}, and nothing in this
paper meets a lower bar than that.

\subsection{Splits}

The 753 cases are partitioned deterministically into 550 test, 100 validation,
and 103 held out, with the test split carrying the main leaderboard. The
validation split was reserved for judge and threshold calibration and, in the end,
was not used: no system was run against it, and we release it unused so that
future work has a split untouched by anything reported here. The held-out split
remained untouched through all training, hyperparameter search, and judge
calibration, and exists to answer a single question: whether the test-split
ordering survives on cases that nothing was optimized against.

One property of these splits should be stated plainly, since it is a defect: they
were drawn per image rather than per patient, and because twenty-two patients
contributed two embryos each, for six of those patients the two embryos fell into
different splits, so that four of the 103 held-out cases share a patient with a
test-split case. We quantify the effect in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}, where it
changes no ranking, but a subsequent version should partition on patient.

\subsection{The prompt}

Every model receives the embryo image together with a structured case
description containing the Gardner grade with plain-language descriptors, the
laboratory fields, and the patient profile, and is asked for five components: a
morphology description, an integration of the patient factors, the risks and
favorable features, a numeric implantation probability, and recommendations for
the present transfer and for future cycles. The full template appears in
Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}.

\subsection{The five rubrics}

A judge model reads the case and the response and returns a 1 to 5 score with a
short justification for each of five rubrics.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Morphological accuracy.} Does the description of the embryo match the
      image and the Gardner annotation?
\item \textbf{Clinical integration.} Does the response connect the patient factors
      to this embryo and this prognosis, rather than listing them?
\item \textbf{Reasoning coherence.} Is the argument internally consistent, with
      uncertainty where uncertainty belongs?
\item \textbf{Guideline alignment.} Is the advice consistent with evidence-based
      reproductive medicine?
\item \textbf{Actionability.} Could a clinician act on this, or is it
      generic?
\end{itemize}

Full rubric text and the 1 to 5 anchors for each appear in
Appendix~\ref{app:rubrics}.

\textbf{The judge.} GPT-5.4 grades every response against the five rubrics in a
single call and additionally extracts the numeric probability, and it is given
exactly what the model was given: the same case text, the embryo image itself,
and the Gardner grade. One refinement matters once an experiment starts
withholding inputs, and Section~\ref{sec:ablation} depends on it. The judge's
copy of the prompt always states which inputs the model actually received, so a
model is never marked down for declining to use something it was denied, while
the judge's own view of the embryo is held constant across arms, because a grader
whose eyesight changes between conditions cannot be used to compare them.

\textbf{How the systems were served.} The three commercial systems ran on Bedrock
and the OpenAI API, our own model on a local vLLM instance, and the four
remaining baselines through OpenRouter, which selects a third-party host per
call. Two consequences of that arrangement bear on how the leaderboard should be
read, and both are quantified in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}: the output-token
budget in Table~\ref{tab:models} was set per backend and is not equal across
systems, and the OpenRouter rows are mixtures of several deployments rather than
single ones.

\textbf{Corrections applied.} Three defects in our own pipeline invalidated
earlier versions of these results and are fixed in everything reported below: the
pipeline used to persist failed judge calls as all-zero scores, our locally
served model was graded on its chain of thought, and the judge was once shown
inputs the model had been denied. Appendix~\ref{app:defects} gives all three in
full. Every score in this paper is produced by the same GPT-5.4 judge with the
embryo in front of it, and no score record contains a judge failure.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}


\subsection{Reading the two outcome metrics}

\textbf{Brier score} (lower is better) is the mean squared error between the
stated probability and the observed outcome, and it rewards honesty about the
odds; always predicting 50\% yields 0.25, which is the comparison most papers
adopt and the wrong one, since the sharper reference is a constant set to the
cohort's own pregnancy rate, against which we report in
Table~\ref{tab:baserate}. \textbf{AUROC} (higher is better, with 0.5 denoting
chance) is the probability that a randomly chosen success is ranked above a
randomly chosen failure, and it rewards discrimination between embryos.
Counseling a patient depends on the first quantity and selecting an embryo for
transfer on the second, and a system can be respectable on one while remaining
useless on the other.

\subsection{Which gaps are real}

A leaderboard reported without intervals invites readers to treat a difference
of 0.02 as meaningful, so we resample cases with replacement 10,000 times and, for
each adjacent pair of systems, bootstrap the paired per-case difference over the
cases both answered.

On the test split every adjacent gap is separable at the 95\% level except the
last, where the base Qwen 9B leads Gemini 2.5 Flash by 0.013 across all the cases
each answered but trails it by 0.006 across the 520 they both answered (95\% CI
$[-0.062, +0.050]$, $p{=}0.83$). The ordering in
Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard} therefore rests entirely on the 30 cases only Gemini
answered, where it averages 2.90 against 3.24 elsewhere, so the pair should be
read as a tie for seventh rather than as a ranking; on the held-out split, with 103 cases rather than 550, the pair
that closes instead is Sonnet 4.6 over Kimi K2.5 ($+0.064$, CI $[-0.019,
+0.153]$, $p{=}0.15$). The two comparisons that carry the paper's argument
survive on both splits: GPT-5.4 over our model ($+0.474$ held out, CI $[+0.414,
+0.538]$) and our model over Opus 4.6 ($+0.146$ held out, CI $[+0.078, +0.214]$;
$+0.205$ on test, CI $[+0.172, +0.237]$). Surviving both splits is not the same
as surviving a second grader, and the second does not
(Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).

\subsection{What the numbers say}

\textbf{1. Case explanation is uniformly strong.} Clinical integration is the
highest-scoring rubric for every system, with GPT-5.4 reaching a maximal 5.00 on
both splits. Models reliably fold age, AMH, endometrial thickness and prior cycle
history into a coherent account of the prognosis, so if the task were confined to
the counseling narrative it would be close to solved.

\textbf{2. Outcome prediction sits at a floor.} AUROC spans 0.456 to 0.562
against 0.5 for chance, so the best value obtained ranks a conceiving embryo
above a non-conceiving one on 56\% of such pairs. The Brier scores look more
favorable than they are: they span 0.241 to 0.289, straddling the 0.25 of always
predicting 50\%, which nine of the sixteen model-split combinations improve on.
Even that is the wrong reference. The clinical pregnancy rate here is 0.350, so a
constant returning 35\% for every case, ignoring the embryo and the patient
entirely, scores 0.2274, and every language model does worse
(Figure~\ref{fig:baserate}).

This is the result we most want retained, and it is not a verdict on the models.
The generated context adds no prognostic information beyond the measured
covariates, and what remains is age, AMH, endometrial thickness, oocyte counts
and one morphology grade from one frame. Predicting clinical pregnancy from that
appears to approach the limit of what public data support, since a logistic
regression on the same fields reaches a cross-validated AUROC of 0.538
(Section~\ref{sec:controls}), inside the range the language models span. What
would move the number is embryo genetics, longitudinal development rather than a
single frame, uterine and endometrial context, and the history that never leaves
the physician's notes. IVF-Bench is built so those fields can be swapped from
generated to measured as they become available.

The same reasoning reframes our own calibration result: a held-out Brier of 0.245
is second of eight, behind Sonnet 4.6 at 0.242, and all eight fall short of the
constant. What post-training achieved is stated probabilities moving back toward
the population rate, correcting the base model's overconfidence, which on these
data is the correct behavior and is not predictive skill.

\textbf{3. Morphology grounding is the weakest rubric, and putting the judge in
front of the image is what revealed it.} Averaged across systems it is lowest on
both splits (2.95 test, 3.00 held out), guideline alignment next (3.28 and 3.31),
clinical integration highest (4.33 and 4.38); no system reaches 3.75 on
morphology on either split, and it is the single weakest rubric for six of eight
systems on test and seven of eight held out. Under the blind judge the same
responses scored 3.39 and 3.44 and morphology was weakest for only two systems on
test and three held out: a judge with no image to check against was accepting
descriptions a sighted judge marks down. Every system loses ground on this rubric
when the judge can see, by 0.13 to 0.65 points and 0.44 on average, while the
largest drop on any other rubric is 0.29 and the average across the other four is
below 0.09, which is what one expects if the correction is specific to image
reading rather than a general increase in severity.
Figure~\ref{fig:rubrics} shows the shape on held out, where clinical integration
is the tallest bar for all eight systems and morphology the shortest for seven.
The conclusion is unchanged in direction and sharper in degree: models reason far
better about structured text than about the image, and the 1.4-point gap between
clinical integration and morphology grounding is the largest separation between
any two rubrics in the study.

\textbf{4. Cost and quality are only loosely coupled.} Opus 4.6 is the most
expensive system at \$170.43 of inference over the 550 test cases, or \$0.310 per
case, and finishes third of eight, while GPT-5.4 finishes first at \$0.090 per
case. Within the Anthropic pair the relation is conventional, Opus at 3.83
against Sonnet at 3.47 for \$0.049 per case, so price tracks quality inside a
family and stops tracking it across families. We exclude Gemini 2.5 Flash, since
four fifths of its answers were cut off by a budget a third the size of the
frontier systems', and our own model, since it ran on hardware we already owned;
Section~\ref{sec:orpo} makes that comparison on a basis that holds.

\subsection{What the embryo image contributes, and why we cannot say}
\label{sec:ablation}

Because each prompt supplies the Gardner grade in text alongside the image, a
system could score well on morphology grounding without consulting the image at
all, which would make the vision component of this benchmark decorative. We set
out to measure that by subtraction, running four systems through the full
two-by-two on the same 100 test cases, withholding the image, the grade, both, or
neither, with the judge shown the embryo in every arm and told which inputs the
model received. The experiment does not answer the question, for a reason that is
a property of the rubric rather than of the models.

\textbf{The arm with nothing in it is not a floor.} A model handed neither input
says it cannot describe an embryo it was not shown, and our rubric scores that
refusal in the middle of the range: the judge's own words are that the model
``appropriately stated that no image was available and did not fabricate embryo
morphology'', followed by a 3 or a 4. Morphology grounding as we wrote it asks
whether a response is accurate, and a response that asserts nothing is not
inaccurate. Three of four systems therefore score higher with nothing than with
both, Gemini by 1.01 points, and no difference between two arms can be read as
the information the missing input carried.

\textbf{What survives.} One edge never involves a refusal, because the model
holds the image in both of its arms: image and grade against image alone. There
the expert summary does measurable work, adding 0.60 points of morphology
grounding for Opus 4.6 (95\% CI $[+0.43, +0.77]$), 0.34 for Gemini 2.5 Flash
($[+0.19, +0.49]$) and 0.23 for Qwen 397B ($[+0.03, +0.42]$), while costing
GPT-5.4 0.13 ($[-0.21, -0.05]$). Each is paired over the cases that pair of arms
shares, 100 everywhere except Qwen 397B at 92, so subtracting columns of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, which uses the 87 common to all four, reproduces them
only to within 0.01. For three of four systems a human reading of the embryo,
compressed into three ordinal values, improves the model's own reading of it.

Separately, GPT-5.4 scores 4.46, 4.49, 4.45 and 4.33 across the four arms:
removing the image and the grade together costs the strongest system in the study
0.13 points of overall score (95\% CI $[+0.07, +0.19]$), so its rubric total is
carried by the patient context almost entirely.

The released benchmark previously carried a figure of 0.07 to 0.23 points for
the image's contribution. That figure was computed under a judge that could not
see the embryo either, and it is withdrawn: this design supports no quantitative
claim in either direction. The defensible statement is narrower: this benchmark
cannot measure image comprehension by subtraction, because its rubrics score a
response against the inputs that produced it rather than against the embryo.
Measuring it needs a reference the judge can compare against, which is what
\citet{neu2026dataset} provide. The paper's broader claim, that the headroom is
in the data rather than in image-only modelling, never rested on this experiment:
it rests on the randomized trial \citep{illingworth2024} and on the tabular
baseline of Section~\ref{sec:controls}.

\subsection{Two controls on the headline claims}
\label{sec:controls}

Two objections bear directly on the results above and we address both with
additional analysis rather than argument.

\textbf{Is the ceiling really the data?} Our claim that the outcome ceiling
reflects the available data rather than model capacity rests, so far, on the
observation that the generated fields add nothing beyond the measured covariates,
which says nothing about what those measured covariates themselves support. We therefore fit logistic regression to the
measured fields alone, namely age, AMH, endometrial thickness, oocytes retrieved,
mature oocytes, transfer day, and the three Gardner components, with missing
values imputed at the column median. Cross-validated within the 550-case split it
reaches AUROC 0.538 (95\% CI $[0.487, 0.586]$), and fit on those 550 cases and
evaluated on the 103 held out it reaches 0.608 (95\% CI $[0.484, 0.725]$), an
interval wide enough to include chance. The better-powered estimate, 0.538, sits
squarely inside the 0.456 to 0.562 range spanned by the vision-language systems,
which is the result that matters: a linear model given the same measured
covariates extracts about as much prognostic signal as the frontier systems do,
so those systems are operating near the limit of what these covariates support
rather than failing to use them.

One qualification follows from the same experiment and cuts against us. On the
held-out split the tabular model attains a Brier score of 0.2197, which does beat
the constant predictor at 0.2274, so the claim that nothing in this study beats
the base rate holds for the language models and not for a fitted tabular model.
The margin is small, the sample is 103 cases, and the cross-validated Brier on
the larger split is 0.2297, which does not beat the constant; we report the
result as it stands rather than choose the split that suits us.

\textbf{Is the post-training margin an artifact of verbosity?} The judge rewards
length, as Section~\ref{sec:robustness} quantifies, and our model was trained
toward a teacher whose responses are long, so the comparison against Opus 4.6
could plausibly be measuring output length. It is not. On the held-out split our
model's responses average 9,485 characters against Opus 4.6's 11,933, so our
model is the shorter of the two, and regressing score on length within each
system and comparing at a common length of 10,709 characters widens the margin
rather than narrowing it, from $+0.146$ raw to $+0.256$ adjusted. The verbosity
confound runs against our result, not for it.

\section{Post-training a small model}
\label{sec:orpo}


The natural question raised by the gap between a 9B open model and a frontier
system is whether that gap reflects a capability ceiling or a deficit of
domain-specific training data, and we tested this directly.

\subsection{Building preference pairs from the benchmark}

For each of the 550 test-split cases we already had scored responses from the
seven baseline systems, or from six on the 30 cases the base 9B model failed to
complete, and we took the highest-scoring as \texttt{chosen} and the
lowest-scoring as \texttt{rejected}, giving 550 pairs with a mean score gap of 1.49 points, of
which 500 were used for training and 50 held for evaluation during the run. We then fine-tuned Qwen 3.5-9B with ORPO
\citep{hong2024orpo}, LoRA rank 8 and alpha 36 applied to all linear layers
including the vision tower, three epochs on two H100s, with the learning rate and
the preference weight chosen by an eight-trial search of one epoch each: it
settled on a learning rate of $7.58\times10^{-4}$ and $\beta = 0.222$, and the
released configuration file carries both.

Three properties of this dataset bear on the interpretation of the result. The
pairs are drawn from the test split, so the trained model is in-domain on the
550-case leaderboard and the held-out split provides the unbiased measurement. The
chosen responses approach a monoculture, since GPT-5.4 authored 460 of the 500
chosen sides in the training split, or 92\%, which means the student is largely
distilling a single teacher that also serves as the judge. And the pairs were
selected by the blind judge, because they were built before we put the judge in
front of the embryo, so which response counts as chosen, the 1.49 gap, and the
92\% all reflect that earlier configuration; the evaluation of the resulting
model, like every score in this paper, uses the sighted judge.

Table~\ref{tab:orpo} gives the result. Five hundred training pairs raised the
model 24.6\% over the base it was fine-tuned from, and the gain is spread across
all five rubrics rather than concentrated in one, which is the result worth emphasizing: the procedure raises
the assessment as a whole instead of optimizing a single reported quantity, and it is reproducible by
anyone with the released dataset and two GPUs. This comparison is the one we
would defend hardest, because it is made between two versions of the same model
on the same cases, so it depends on no convention about how systems from
different laboratories should be ranked against each other, and
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} confirms it holds at 15.5\% under a judge from a
different model family.

Two things differ between the arms besides training, and we held neither fixed.
The base model was decoded greedily like every other baseline, while ours uses
the sampling the Qwen 3.5 card recommends, since greedy decoding traps it in a
repetition loop; greedy decoding does the base no favours either, and on 30 test
cases it spent the whole budget without emitting an answer. And ours ran on a
vLLM instance we controlled while the base was reached through OpenRouter, served
across its 623 calls by Together on 414, Venice on 117 and DeepInfra on 28, with
no host named on the remaining 64, so that arm is a mixture of three deployments
compared against a single one of ours. Neither difference can be separated from
the training effect after the fact, so some unknown part of the 24.6\% belongs to
them. The clean experiment is to decode both identically on one serving stack; we
did not run it.

The same gain moves the model from last on the held-out split to second, ahead of
Claude Opus 4.6. We report that because it is what the leaderboard says, subject
to the two qualifications Section~\ref{sec:robustness} establishes: the second
place is GPT-5.4's verdict and becomes fifth under Sonnet 4.6, and our model was
truncated on 12 held-out cases where Opus was not.

The Brier improvement in that table should be read alongside
Table~\ref{tab:baserate}, which puts every system behind a constant predictor.
What the fine-tune fixed is not predictive skill but overconfidence: the base
model's mean stated probability was 0.451 against a true rate of 0.350, and the
trained model's is 0.396. Whether the same recipe yields real predictive skill
when the context fields are measured rather than generated is the open question
this benchmark exists to make answerable.

The base model's AUROC differs sharply between splits, 0.556 on test against
0.456 on held out, but with roughly 36 positive cases in the held-out split the
standard error on that statistic is near 0.06, so the difference is not
distinguishable from sampling noise and we draw no inference from it.

The cost comparison deferred from Section~\ref{sec:results} belongs here,
because the two systems are not billed on the same basis: Opus 4.6 costs \$0.310
per case through its API, whereas our model was served on a two-GPU instance we
already operated, so its marginal cost is GPU time rather than a per-token price.
At 19 seconds per case on the \$8.38 per hour instance used throughout this
study, that is \$0.044 per case, roughly one seventh of the Opus figure, and it
falls further under batching. We report both bases rather than the single ratio, since the
seven-fold figure is the defensible one and a comparison against our recorded
\$8.40, which is entirely judge cost, would not be.

\section{What the benchmark itself gets wrong}
\label{sec:robustness}


A benchmark paper should attempt to falsify its own instrument. We report seven
checks, six of which reuse the collected responses and cost nothing. The first
two concern experimental conditions that were not equalised, and they bear on how
every row of Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard} should be read.

\textbf{Were the systems served comparably?} No. The four baselines reached
through OpenRouter were served by whichever third-party host the router picked:
15 distinct providers across the 653 Kimi K2.5 calls, 11 across the 653 for Qwen
3.5-397B, and 3 across the 559 named of 623 for the base Qwen 3.5-9B. Gemini 2.5
Flash named a host on only 103 of its 653, so for that row we cannot say how
mixed it was. Providers differ in quantisation, sampling implementation and chat
templating, so those rows are weighted mixtures of deployments rather than single
systems measured repeatedly. The Bedrock, OpenAI and local vLLM rows are not. We
release the per-case provider manifest.

\textbf{Did every system get to finish?} No, and the cause is our harness rather
than the models. The generation budget was set per backend and the three settings
were not equal: Bedrock and OpenAI allowed 12,000 output tokens, the OpenRouter
path 4,096. The three systems that never hit their ceiling, GPT-5.4,
Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6, are exactly the three on the larger budget, and all five
on the smaller one were cut off mid-answer on some cases. Gemini is extreme for a
second reason: its hidden reasoning counts inside the 4,096, consuming roughly
3,000 tokens and leaving about 1,100 for an answer the rubric then scored as
incomplete. Table~\ref{tab:truncation} bounds what that cost each system.

its 550 test calls and Google on all 103 held-out ones, so the two runs were not
collected the same way. The confound also runs against our own headline
comparison, since our model was cut off on 74 test and 12 held-out cases where
Opus 4.6 never was. We report the leaderboard as collected; the fix is one budget
large enough that nothing reaches it.

\textbf{Do the five rubrics measure five distinct constructs?} Only partly. The
mean correlation between distinct rubrics is 0.48 and the first principal
component takes 59\% of the variance across 4,370 judgments, so there is a
substantial general quality factor. It is not degenerate: recommendation
usefulness correlates with clinical integration at 0.70 but with guideline
alignment at 0.30, which justifies reporting the five separately while cautioning
against reading their mean as five independent measurements.

\textbf{Does the judge reward verbosity?} Unevenly. Pooled across systems the
Spearman correlation between length and overall score is 0.63; per system it runs
from $-0.02$ for Opus 4.6 to $+0.78$ for Gemini 2.5 Flash, whose extreme value
has a mundane cause, since four fifths of its answers were cut off and length
there is largely a proxy for finishing. The pooled figure overstates the
within-system effect because it also absorbs stronger systems writing more, and
for five of eight systems the correlation is below 0.25.

\textbf{Does patient-level leakage affect the results?} No. Twenty-two patients
contributed two embryos each, and keeping one per patient removes 16 test cases
without changing any ranking or moving any score by more than 0.01.

\textbf{How much did showing the judge the image change the answer?} Our first
configuration had the judge blind, so morphology grounding could only measure
consistency with the Gardner grade in text and a fluent description of an embryo
that was not there would have passed unremarked. Every score here comes from the
sighted configuration instead, and we hold the blind scores for the same 5,194
responses. Absolute scores fall, concentrated in morphology grounding, while the
ordering barely moves: Spearman correlation between the rankings is 0.95 on test
and 0.98 on held out, and the top four are identical on both splits. Of three
single-place swaps below that, two are between pairs the bootstrap cannot
separate. The third is a genuine disagreement: on test the blind judge puts Kimi
above Sonnet by 0.052 and the sighted judge puts Sonnet above Kimi by 0.083, both
intervals excluding zero. Our own margin over Opus 4.6 is wider under the sighted
judge, $+0.205$ against $+0.161$ on test and $+0.146$ against $+0.103$ held out,
so the correction that most threatened our headline comparison strengthened it.
We regard the sighted configuration as correct on principle rather than on that
outcome: a rubric asking whether a description matches the embryo cannot be
scored by a grader who has not seen it.

\textbf{Does judge self-preference inflate our result?} It does. We re-scored all
824 held-out judgements with Claude Sonnet 4.6, a judge from a different family
that wrote none of the training data, giving it the same embryo image so that the
comparison isolates the judge rather than its eyesight. The two agree at the ends
and disagree in the middle: GPT-5.4 is first and the base Qwen 9B last under
both, but the rank correlation is 0.55 ($p{=}0.16$) and our model moves from
second to fifth. The disagreement is structured. Sonnet scores every system
higher by 0.50 on average, but the two Anthropic systems gain most, 0.90 and
0.79, while the two systems in the GPT-5.4 lineage gain least, 0.18 and 0.16;
those four take the extremes of a ranking by gain, and verbosity does not explain
it, since gain and mean response length correlate at $-0.14$. Our headline
comparison sits on the seam: we lead Opus 4.6 by $+0.146$ (95\% CI $[+0.078,
+0.214]$) under GPT-5.4 and trail it by $-0.485$ ($[-0.575, -0.404]$) under
Sonnet 4.6. Table~\ref{tab:crossjudge} ranks the systems by how much the second

release both sets of transcripts.

\section{Four things we learned about grading with a language model}
\label{sec:judgelessons}


Two of these are the pipeline defects of Section~\ref{sec:setup}; the other two
are properties of rubric-based judging that careful implementation does not
remove. Each affected a result in this study before it was caught, and each will
recur in any benchmark of this shape.

\textbf{Tell the judge what the model was given, and change nothing else about
the judge.} Break the first half and the judge penalises a model for correctly
declining to use an input it never received. Obey only the first half and the
judge goes blind in exactly the arms where the model did, which makes the arms
incomparable.

\textbf{A rubric conditioned on the response cannot measure the input.} A model
that declines to describe an embryo it was not given is not inaccurate, so
subtractive ablation is impossible on such a rubric. Measuring what an input
contributes needs a reference to compare against, not the absence of the input.

\textbf{Judges favour their own family, symmetrically.} Neither ordering is
neutral. A leaderboard built on one judge should say whose leaderboard it is.

\textbf{Set the generation budget once, in one place, and check it.} Ours was
configured per backend, the settings disagreed, and nothing in the scores
revealed it. What reads as a capability gap can be an accounting one.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}


\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The judge has no reference description to compare the embryo
      against}, so morphology grounding weighs one model's reading against
      another's. The expert descriptions of \citet{neu2026dataset} are the
      remedy.
\item \textbf{One judge, and it is also a contestant.} GPT-5.4 grades a
      leaderboard it appears on and wrote 92\% of our training targets;
      Section~\ref{sec:robustness} shows a second judge reorders the middle. A
      third would establish which ordering is closer to consensus.
\item \textbf{The generation budget was not equal across systems}, and the
      OpenRouter rows are provider mixtures. Both are quantified in
      Section~\ref{sec:robustness} and both favour the three commercial systems.
\item \textbf{The base and trained models differ in decoding and serving stack}
      as well as in training, so part of the 24.6\% may be either
      (Section~\ref{sec:orpo}).
\item \textbf{The prompt asks for implantation probability and we score against
      clinical pregnancy.} Treat the outcome metrics as a stress test rather
      than a matched forecast.
\item \textbf{Part of the patient context is generated}, which caps what any
      outcome metric here can show and omits real inter-field correlations
      (Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}).
\item \textbf{One clinic, one scanner, one population}, splits drawn per image
      rather than per patient, and 550 test cases against 103 held out. Enough to
      separate most models, not close ones, which is why every comparison carries
      an interval.
\end{itemize}

\section{Rubric definitions}
\label{app:rubrics}
The judge returns a score and a one-line justification for each rubric, plus the numeric probability it extracted from the response. The text below is what the judge sees, verbatim.

\paragraph{Morphological Accuracy}
How accurately does the response describe the embryo's morphological features as visible in the image? Consider: expansion stage assessment, ICM description, TE description, zona pellucida observations, overall blastocyst appearance.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{1}: Major errors or fabricated features not visible in the image
\item \textbf{2}: Several inaccuracies in morphological description
\item \textbf{3}: Mostly accurate with some minor errors or omissions
\item \textbf{4}: Accurate description with good detail
\item \textbf{5}: Precise, thorough morphological analysis matching the image
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Clinical Integration}
How well does the response integrate patient clinical factors (age, AMH, BMI, diagnosis, previous outcomes, sperm parameters) with the embryo assessment? Does it consider how these factors interact with embryo quality?
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{1}: No integration of clinical factors with embryo assessment
\item \textbf{2}: Mentions clinical factors but doesn't connect them to embryo quality
\item \textbf{3}: Some integration but misses key interactions
\item \textbf{4}: Good integration of most relevant clinical factors
\item \textbf{5}: Comprehensive integration showing how clinical factors affect prognosis
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reasoning Coherence}
Is the clinical reasoning logical, consistent, and free of contradictions? Does the probability estimate follow from the evidence presented? Are conclusions supported by the observations?
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{1}: Contradictory or illogical reasoning
\item \textbf{2}: Significant logical gaps or inconsistencies
\item \textbf{3}: Generally coherent with minor logical issues
\item \textbf{4}: Well-structured reasoning with clear logic
\item \textbf{5}: Exemplary reasoning chain with all conclusions well-supported
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Guideline Alignment}
Does the response align with established IVF clinical guidelines (ESHRE, ASRM, Gardner grading criteria)? Are the recommendations consistent with evidence-based reproductive medicine?
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{1}: Recommendations contradict established guidelines
\item \textbf{2}: Several recommendations not aligned with guidelines
\item \textbf{3}: Mostly aligned with some deviations
\item \textbf{4}: Well-aligned with current clinical guidelines
\item \textbf{5}: Fully aligned, demonstrates deep knowledge of current guidelines
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Actionability}
Are the recommendations specific, practical, and useful for a clinician? Does the response provide clear next steps for this transfer and future cycle planning? Are recommendations appropriately nuanced?
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{1}: No actionable recommendations or only generic advice
\item \textbf{2}: Vague recommendations with little practical value
\item \textbf{3}: Some useful recommendations but lacking specificity
\item \textbf{4}: Clear, specific recommendations for this case
\item \textbf{5}: Highly specific, nuanced recommendations with alternatives considered
\end{itemize}

\section{Three evaluation defects}
\label{app:defects}
We report all three because each is easy to reproduce inadvertently, none is
visible in the resulting scores, and the third produced a published-looking
result that was entirely an artifact.

\textbf{Failed judge calls were persisted.} When a call errored the pipeline
wrote a score record of five zeros and continued, and because later runs skip
cases that already have a record, those failures became permanent. In our initial
pass this silently invalidated 231 test-split scores, 211 of them on a single
model, so that model's row was computed from 309 of 550 cases while the table
said 520. The judge now declines to persist a failed score.

\textbf{Reasoning was graded as answer.} Our model was served through vLLM
without a reasoning parser, and because Qwen chat templates pre-fill
\texttt{<think>} the model emits its reasoning, a closing \texttt{</think>} and
then its answer in one field, whereas hosted APIs return reasoning separately.
Ours was graded on roughly 4,000 characters of unfiltered reasoning prepended to
its answer while every other system was graded on the answer alone; correcting
this raised its test score by 0.36 points and moved it from third to second.
Comparisons between locally served reasoning models and hosted APIs require
checking what is actually in the string being graded.

\textbf{The judge saw inputs the model had been denied.} This occurred twice, on
two different fields. We first ran the image
ablation with the judge reading the stored prompt, which still said
\texttt{[IMAGE ATTACHED]}, while the model in the withheld arm had been told
otherwise. The models behaved correctly and said they could not describe an
embryo they had not seen; the judge, believing an image was attached, marked that
down. GPT-5.4's morphology score appeared to fall from 3.94 to 1.98, and 35 of 40
sampled justifications cited the missing description as the reason. Read at face
value it was a striking demonstration that the vision channel carries substantial
information. It was an artifact of our own prompt handling. Having fixed the
image marker we ran the two-by-two and reproduced the same mistake on the Gardner
grade: arms that withheld the grade from the model still showed it to the judge,
whose justifications then objected that a description ``conflicts with the
provided lab grade of 1DD'' about a grade the model never received. On one
representative case morphology grounding moved from 2 to 4 once the judge's copy
matched the model's.

Both were fixed in one place. The judge now derives its copy of the prompt from
the run metadata recording what the model was given. Fixing only the text would
have introduced a third asymmetry, this time in the judge, since a judge denied
the image in one arm and given it in another is a different instrument in each;
in the ablation arms the embryo is therefore still attached to the judge call
even when the model was denied it, together with an instruction that the model
could not see it and must not be penalised for declining to describe it.

All results in this paper follow all three corrections: every score is produced
by GPT-5.4 with the embryo in front of it, no score record contains a judge
failure, and in every arm the judge was told exactly what the model had been
given. The confounded score sets ship alongside the corrected ones, under
directory names identifying the confound.
